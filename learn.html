The philosophy before the formulas
Lesson 01 Why I Built CFP Advantage

What this answers

Why do good teams actually look good, and why do some final scores fail to explain the game?

The idea

College football fans already know the scoreboard matters. The problem is that it often leaves out the shape of the game. A team can win by three touchdowns without feeling dominant, or lose a close game after controlling long stretches of the afternoon.

CFP Advantage was built to ask a different question: not just who won, but why the game moved the way it did. The model starts with drive-level football instead of reputation, polls, or one final margin.

Football example

Two teams both score 35. One creates long, repeatable scoring drives. The other scores on a return, a short field, and one broken play. The scoreboard matches, but the football profile does not.

How to use it

Read CFP Advantage as a football explanation system first. Rankings, projections, and profiles all come from the same question: what did the team consistently prove on the field?

Lesson 02 What Is Football Advantage?

What this answers

When does a possession become more than just yards or time of possession?

The idea

Advantage is useful value before the scoreboard speaks. It can show up as an early first down, better field position, staying ahead of schedule, or forcing the defense into choices it does not want to make.

CFP Advantage cares less about empty movement and more about whether the offense created a drive state that actually threatened the opponent.

Football example

A seven-yard gain on first down and a third-and-one conversion may not look explosive, but together they change the drive. The offense is now in control of the possession.

Important caution

Advantage is not the same as total yards. A drive can gain yards late, behind schedule, or after the game state has already changed.

Lesson 03 Why The Box Score Can Lie

What this answers

Why can two similar box scores describe two completely different football games?

The idea

Box scores compress football into totals. They tell you how many yards, points, first downs, and turnovers happened. They do not always tell you where those plays happened, how repeatable the success was, or whether the offense was actually in command.

CFP Advantage keeps the familiar stats, then places them beside drive-level context so the user can see whether the scoreboard confirmed the performance or hid something underneath it.

How to use it

Use traditional stats as comparison evidence, not the entire answer. The Contextual Football Profile shows the deeper shape behind those totals.

Lesson 04 What Is Control?

What this answers

When has an offense created meaningful pressure on a drive?

The idea

Control is not simply having the ball. It starts when a drive becomes dangerous in a football sense: the offense earns an early first down, stays ahead of schedule, improves field position, and forces the defense to respond.

The model found a coach-friendly pattern: meaningful control usually looks like good football, not a random gift.

91-93%

of meaningful control drives earned an early first down.

62-67%

included an explosive play, but explosives alone did not define control.

~14%

started on a short field, so most control was earned rather than gifted.

Important caution

Control is a drive-quality idea. It does not mean the team scored, and it does not mean the drive was perfect.

Offensive Control

How offenses create and convert pressure
Lesson 05 Control Creation

What this answers

Can this offense keep putting the defense in trouble?

The idea

Control Creation measures how often an offense turns possessions into meaningful control drives. It is a foundation metric because a team cannot consistently finish opportunities it never creates.

This is why a team with a perfect finish rate can still be limited if it only creates a few real chances. Creation tells you how much opportunity the offense manufactures.

How to read it on CFP Advantage

On team and matchup pages, stronger Control Creation means the offense is more consistently building drives that put pressure on the opponent.

Lesson 06 Control Finish Rate

What this answers

When the offense creates meaningful control, does it turn those chances into points?

Formula

Control Finish Rate = scoring control drives / meaningful control drives.

The idea

Control Finish Rate is not red-zone efficiency. Red zone efficiency begins with field location. Control Finish Rate begins with drive quality. It asks what happened after the offense established meaningful control.

The two are related, but they are not the same. In CFP Advantage audits, Control Finish Rate had only a moderate relationship with red-zone scoring rate and red-zone touchdown rate.

~0.39

correlation with red-zone scoring rate.

~0.35

correlation with red-zone touchdown rate.

Important caution

A high finish rate needs sample context. Finishing 2 of 2 control drives is not the same as finishing 8 of 10.

Lesson 07 Control Points Per Offensive Drive

What this answers

How much sustainable scoring pressure does this offense create each time it gets the ball?

Bridge

Control Points Per Offensive Drive = Control Creation Rate x Points Per Control Drive.

The idea

This is not literal scoreboard points per drive. It is an ADV-derived pressure estimate that spreads control-created scoring value across every offensive possession.

A value around 3.5 does not mean the team scores a touchdown every drive. A touchdown every drive would be near 7.0. A 3.5 means elite sustained pressure: across ten offensive drives, the offense is creating roughly 35 control-pressure points.

Under 1.5Limited
1.5-2.3Average
2.4-3.0Strong
3.1+Elite

How to use it

Compare it with points per game. Pressure is the engine; points are the result. When they disagree, the scoreboard may be running hotter or colder than the team's underlying control profile.
